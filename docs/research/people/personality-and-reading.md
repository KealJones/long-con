# Personality frameworks and reading people: what to trust

Research note for Beep's person notes. The question: which personality models and people-reading techniques are solid enough to shape what an assistant stores and does, and which are pseudoscience? Beep's rule, that an empty packet beats a wrong one, turns out to match what the literature supports.

**Bottom line.** Trait *dimensions* measured by validated questionnaires (Big Five, HEXACO, attachment anxiety/avoidance) are real and fairly stable. Even so, they predict any single moment weakly (around r = .30). Type systems (MBTI, DISC, Enneagram, love languages, learning styles) range from weak to unsupported. Inferring traits from text works only modestly (r ≈ .2 to .4, at best ~.56 with large behavioral datasets). People, LLMs included, are bad at reading lies and emotions from surface cues. The safe kind of tailoring follows what a person *said* they want. The risky kind targets traits the system *inferred*.

## 1. Frameworks

| Framework | What it claims | Evidence strength | Key numbers | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Big Five / FFM** | Five continuous trait dimensions (O, C, E, A, N) | **Strong** | ~87% of published trait-outcome links replicated, though effects were usually smaller than first reported (63-71% smaller, 30-42% substantially smaller). Rank-order stability rises from .31 (childhood) to .54 (college), .64 (age 30), and plateaus at ~.74 (ages 50-70). **Types vs traits in general.** Knowing someone's type adds "little or nothing" to prediction beyond the continuous traits that define the type ([Funder ch.5](https://nerd.wwnorton.com/ebooks/epub/puzzle9/EPUB/content/2.2.4-chapter05.xhtml), citing Costa et al. 2002 and McCrae et al. 2006). For data modeling, store dimensions and evidence, never type labels.

### How much does a trait predict a single moment?

- **The "personality coefficient."** Mischel (1968) argued that trait-to-behavior correlations rarely exceed ~.30-.40. Funder & Ozer (1983) showed that classic *situational* effects are about the same size, and a broad review put the typical social-psych effect at r = .21 ([Funder ch.4](https://nerd.wwnorton.com/ebooks/epub/puzzle9/EPUB/content/2.1.3-chapter04.xhtml)). An r of .40 means being right ~70% of the time where chance is 50%: useful, but far from reliable for one prediction.
- **Aggregation is the resolution.** Single acts are noisy, but averages over many occasions are stable (Epstein 1979, [same chapter](https://nerd.wwnorton.com/ebooks/epub/puzzle9/EPUB/content/2.1.3-chapter04.xhtml)).
- **Density distributions.** In experience-sampling studies, a typical person's momentary states varied about as much as the whole sample did. Yet the *center* of each person's distribution was stable week to week (r ≈ .8), its spread less so (.5-.7), and its shape least (.2-.5) ([Fleeson & Jayawickreme, Whole Trait Theory](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4472377)). Translated: "Sam is usually blunt" can be a fine note. "Sam will be blunt in this message" is a guess.

## 2. Reading people: what's observable and how accurate

**Thin slices.** Ambady & Rosenthal's meta-analysis of 44 studies (38 independent results) found that judgments from brief behavior clips predicted outcomes at mean r = .39. Clips under 30 seconds were no worse than 5-minute ones ([Ambady & Rosenthal 1992](https://core.ac.uk/download/283867479.pdf)). So brief observation carries real but modest signal, and more of the *same* kind of exposure doesn't add much.

**Zero-acquaintance accuracy and the Realistic Accuracy Model (RAM).** Funder's RAM says accuracy requires that relevant cues be *available*, that the judge *detect* them, and that the judge *use* them correctly. Four moderators shape the result: the good judge, the good target, the good trait, and good information ([Funder 2012, Current Directions](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/current-directions/0963721412445309/)). Findings collected in [Funder's textbook ch.4](https://nerd.wwnorton.com/ebooks/epub/puzzle9/EPUB/content/2.1.4-chapter04.xhtml):
- Strangers who sat silently for 15 minutes rated extraversion, conscientiousness, and openness at r > .30.
- **Extraversion is judged far more accurately than neuroticism.** Extraversion, conscientiousness, and intelligence could be judged within 5 seconds. Neuroticism, openness, and agreeableness needed minutes.
- Targets vary more in how "judgable" they are than judges vary in skill. Coherent, consistent, well-adjusted people are easier to read.
- More information raises accuracy. Over 5→30 minutes of video, accuracy rose from ~.21 to .27 while consensus between judges stayed flat. Close acquaintances agree with self-reports far better than strangers.

**Self-other knowledge asymmetry (SOKA).** The self knows internal traits like anxiety better. Friends know intellect and creativity better. Extraversion is judged about equally by self, friends, and strangers. Evaluative traits are hard for the self to judge ([Vazire 2010 via WashU](https://source.washu.edu/2010/03/others-may-know-us-better-than-we-know-ourselves-study-finds/)). For notes on *other* people, an observer can get decent signal on visible traits and very little on inner states.

**Digital footprints.** With 86,220 people, models built on Facebook Likes predicted self-reported Big Five at r = .56, beating friends' questionnaire judgments at r = .49. About 90-100 Likes were enough to equal an average human judge ([Youyou, Kosinski & Stillwell 2015, PNAS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25583507/)). Language models trained on 66,732 users' posts agreed with self-reports and were stable over 6 months ([Park et al. 2015, JPSP](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/automatic-personality-assessment-through-social-media-language)). These results come from *large* behavioral datasets and supervised models, not a few chat turns.

**LLMs inferring personality from text (2023-2026):**
- Zero-shot GPT inference from Facebook status updates: average r = .29 (range .22-.33), about as good as purpose-trained supervised models. It was *more accurate for women and younger users*, which is a bias ([Peters & Matz 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08631v1)).
- From 518 semi-structured interviews, zero-shot and chain-of-thought prompting topped out at r < .26, and some correlations were near zero or negative. Chain-of-thought added little ([arXiv 2509.13244](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.13244v1)). The journal version found *supervised fine-tuning* reached r ≈ .4-.7 for conscientiousness, agreeableness, and neuroticism. Prompting GPT-5 mini peaked at r = .158, and openness was near zero or negative throughout ([Zhu et al. 2026, JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e87129)).
- Takeaway: an LLM prompted to "read" someone's personality from chat is about as accurate as a stranger, unevenly across traits and demographic groups.

**Lie detection.** Across 206 documents and 24,483 judges, people averaged 54% correct, classifying 47% of lies and 61% of truths correctly ([Bond & DePaulo 2006](https://assets.aclu.org/live/uploads/document/foia/2006-Personality-and-Social-Psychology-Review-Accuracy-of-Deception-Judgements.pdf)). That's barely above chance, with a bias toward believing truth.

**Micro-expressions and body language.** In a controlled test, Ekman-style METT micro-expression training did no better than bogus training or none, and overall accuracy was "slightly below chance" ([Jordan et al. 2019](https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/a-test-of-the-micro-expressions-training-tool-does-it-improve-lie/)). Treat "tells" as folklore.

**Emotion from faces.** In a major review, facial configurations for basic emotions showed weak reliability (roughly the 20-39% band) and poor specificity: a scowl often means something other than anger. The authors warn against "reverse inference" from face to emotion and recommend descriptive labels ("frown") over emotion labels ("sad") ([Barrett et al. 2019, PSPI](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6640856)). The text equivalent: "wrote in short, curt messages" is an observation, "was angry" is an inference.

**Fundamental attribution error / correspondence bias.** People readily infer "unique and enduring dispositions" from behavior that the situation fully explains ([Gilbert & Malone 1995](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/danielgilbert/files/gilbert__malone_correspondence_bias.pdf)). An assistant summarizing "Sam was rude in this thread" into "Sam is rude" makes exactly this error, and then keeps re-serving it.

## 3. Tailoring: does matching messages to personality help?

**Psychological targeting.** Matz et al. (2017) reached 3.5M Facebook users. Ads matched to personality inferred from a single Like produced up to 40% more clicks and 50% more purchases ([Cambridge JBS summary](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/insight/2017/how-effective-is-psychological-targeting-in-advertising/)). **Critique:** Eckles, Gordon & Johnson (2018, PNAS) argued the field designs had internal-validity threats that could confound targeting with outcomes, so the causal claim isn't clean ([letter](https://brgordon1.github.io/publication/2018-psychologically-targeted-ads)).

**LLM-personalized persuasion:**
- Matz et al. 2024, *Scientific Reports*: four studies, N = 1,788. ChatGPT-written messages tailored to psychological profiles were "significantly more" influential than generic ones, for example raising willingness to pay for a getaway by $117 ([paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-53755-0.pdf)).
- Hackenburg & Margetts 2024, *PNAS*: LLM microtargeting of political messages on 10 sociodemographic attributes was *not* meaningfully more persuasive than generic messages. In their reply to Teeny & Matz, both sides agree the null isn't proof of impossibility ([reply](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11513937/)).
- Salvi et al. 2025, *Nature Human Behaviour*: in live debates, GPT-4 given only basic demographics about its opponent beat humans in 64.4% of unequal pairs, an 81.2% relative increase in the odds of shifting agreement ([abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nathum/v9y2025i8d10.1038_s41562-025-02194-6.html); [preprint, N = 820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14380v1)).
- Net: personalization effects are real but inconsistent and usually small. Where they appear, they come with clear manipulation risk, and the strongest evidence comes from *persuasion* settings, not helpfulness.

**Language style matching (LSM).** Matching in function words predicted mutual interest in speed dates (33% vs 9% above/below median) and couples still together at 3 months (77% vs 54%) ([Ireland et al. 2011, Psych Science](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/IrelandEtAl2011_RelationshipPrediction.pdf)). This is *correlational*: LSM marks rapport that already exists, and engineering it is not shown to create rapport.

**Risk gradient for an assistant:**
- *Low risk, well supported:* following preferences the person *stated* (length, directness, swearing OK, "challenge me", visuals), and mirroring register within a conversation. These need no trait inference, and errors are corrected cheaply in one turn.
- *Medium risk:* storing *observed, situated* behavior with dates ("short replies late at night, 3×"). This is useful only in aggregate.
- *High risk:* inferring traits, emotions, attachment style, or type labels about the user or third parties and steering messages by them. Accuracy is ~.2-.3, the inferences are biased by demographic group, they invite the fundamental attribution error, and personalized persuasion is the exact capability flagged as a manipulation risk.

## Implications for an assistant's person notes

1. **Store what was said and observed, not what you inferred.** "Said they hate wrap-up questions" (stated) is high-quality. "Is avoidant" (inferred) is a stranger-level guess at ~r = .2-.3 ([Peters & Matz](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08631v1), [Zhu et al.](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e87129)). An empty field beats this.
2. **Never store type labels** (MBTI, DISC, Enneagram, love language, learning style, attachment "style", zodiac). They are unreliable (MBTI ~50% retype in 5 weeks) or unsupported, and they add nothing over dimensions ([Funder ch.5](https://nerd.wwnorton.com/ebooks/epub/puzzle9/EPUB/content/2.2.4-chapter05.xhtml)). If the user *self-describes* with one ("I'm an INTJ lol"), record it as a self-description, not a fact.
3. **Describe, don't diagnose.** Use the Barrett rule: "sent short replies" rather than "was annoyed." Emotion-from-cues and lie-detection accuracy are near chance ([Barrett 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6640856), [Bond & DePaulo](https://assets.aclu.org/live/uploads/document/foia/2006-Personality-and-Social-Psychology-Review-Accuracy-of-Deception-Judgements.pdf)).
4. **Tag every behavioral note with situation and date, and require repetition before generalizing.** One instance is a state. Only the center of a person's distribution is stable ([Fleeson](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4472377)). This guards against the correspondence bias ([Gilbert & Malone](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/danielgilbert/files/gilbert__malone_correspondence_bias.pdf)).
5. **Be most skeptical about third parties' inner states.** Observers read extraversion-like, visible traits reasonably well and neuroticism or anxiety poorly ([Funder ch.4](https://nerd.wwnorton.com/ebooks/epub/puzzle9/EPUB/content/2.1.4-chapter04.xhtml), [Vazire 2010](https://source.washu.edu/2010/03/others-may-know-us-better-than-we-know-ourselves-study-finds/)). The assistant only ever sees the *user's account* of other people, which makes it a second-hand judge.
6. **Prefer facts that are good for retrieval:** commitments, preferences, relationships, dates, recurring topics, things the person asked to be reminded of. These are verifiable and cheap to correct. Personality is neither.
7. **Tailor to stated preferences only** (length, tone, directness, format). Treat inferred-trait tailoring as out of scope. Effects are small and inconsistent ([Hackenburg & Margetts](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11513937/)), and when they work they're persuasion ([Salvi 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nathum/v9y2025i8d10.1038_s41562-025-02194-6.html), [Matz 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-53755-0.pdf)).
8. **Keep notes visible and correctable.** Self-other agreement improves with acquaintance and information ([Funder ch.4](https://nerd.wwnorton.com/ebooks/epub/puzzle9/EPUB/content/2.1.4-chapter04.xhtml)). Let the person be the ground truth and overwrite the assistant.
9. **Big Five is the only trait vocabulary worth borrowing, and only as a *lens*, not a stored score.** It's useful for knowing which behaviors are diagnostic (talkativeness → extraversion), with the ~.30 ceiling on any single prediction kept in mind ([Funder ch.4](https://nerd.wwnorton.com/ebooks/epub/puzzle9/EPUB/content/2.1.3-chapter04.xhtml)).
10. **Watch demographic skew.** LLM trait inference is more accurate for some groups than others ([Peters & Matz](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08631v1)), so inferred notes will be systematically worse for some people in the user's life.
11. **Rapport signals are outcomes, not levers.** LSM tracks rapport ([Ireland 2011](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/IrelandEtAl2011_RelationshipPrediction.pdf)). Natural register-matching is fine, but don't engineer it as a persuasion tactic, especially in drafts written to third parties.
12. **If a fact would be sensitive in the wrong packet** (a third party's health or relationship troubles), the wrong-packet cost is high and the inference value is low. Default to leaving it out unless the user stated it and it's relevant to the turn.

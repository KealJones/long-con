# What is worth remembering about a person (and what isn't)

Research for Long Con's people cards and dated interest notes. Every source below was read as a page, not just a search snippet. Tags: **[peer-reviewed]** (journal or conference paper), **[preprint]** (arXiv, not yet reviewed or venue unconfirmed), **[vendor]** (written by the company selling the product), **[practitioner]** (trade blog or guide).

---

## 1. How skilled humans keep notes on people

### Salespeople: the Mackay 66
- Harvey Mackay's 66-question customer profile covers education, family (marital status, children's names), anniversary, hobbies, favourite sports teams, vacation habits, previous jobs, clubs and associations, what the person feels strongly about, what they're proudest of, and the status symbols in their office. The aim is "the person behind the desk." [practitioner] ([harveymackay.com](https://harveymackay.com/knowing-something-about-your-customer-is-just-as-important-as-knowing-your-product/))
- How it's filled in matters as much as what's in it. Facts come from **ordinary conversation and observation**, get **added to at every interaction**, and the profile **doesn't have to be complete**: "30 is better than 20." (same source)
- Mackay on custody: salespeople "guard this information with our lives, being very sensitive to how we use it and who has access"; it is "not office gossip." [practitioner] ([Inc.](https://www.inc.com/harvey-mackay/66-ways-to-know-your-customer-your-real-customer.html))

### Executive assistants: the dossier and the preference doc
- A typical EA "executive dossier" covers communication style, travel preferences, food allergies and medical information, spouse's details, anniversary, children's names and birthdays, memberships and usual lunch orders. It also holds IDs, cards and passwords, which is why the author keeps it "UNDER LOCK AND KEY." Gather information "at their comfort level," since some executives share everything at once and others need to build trust first. [practitioner] ([IAAP Edge](https://edge.iaap-hq.org/2016/04/19/executive-dossier/))
- Preference docs also record working style: preferred channel (Slack or WhatsApp), how they like a conversation to end, pace, and time orientation. Fill them by **observing, asking, then writing it down**, and revisit them "to clarify or realign expectations." [practitioner] ([EA Campus](https://theeacampus.com/blog/executive-preferences-priorities-and-pet-peeves/))

### Hospitality: Ritz-Carlton
- Staff carry a "guest preference pad" as part of the uniform, or phone a hotline. Notes go to a **guest recognition manager** at each hotel, who **reviews them before they enter** the CLASS system. Examples given are smoking and bed type, plus name, favourite table and favourite room. [practitioner/trade press] ([destinationCRM](https://www.destinationcrm.com/Articles/CRM-News/CRM-Featured-Articles/For-Ritz-Carlton-It-All-Begins-with-Customer-Knowledge-47424.aspx))
- The design point: many people capture, one curator commits. Raw sightings are not profile facts until someone reviews them.

### Clinicians: what goes in the record and what stays out
- A medical **social history** records substance use, occupation, sexual behaviour (for infection risk), travel, exercise, diet, firearms at home, education, living conditions and social support. Each item is there because it affects care. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_history_(medicine)))
- Therapists keep **two records**. Progress notes (diagnoses, treatment plans, results) are shared with the care team. Psychotherapy notes (the therapist's impressions) are kept apart and get stronger HIPAA protection. Notes should be "specific, concise, and non-judgmental." [practitioner] ([Palo Alto U.](https://paloaltou.edu/resources/business-of-practice-blog/therapy-notes))
- When patients can read their notes, **10.5% of 22,959** said something in them made them feel judged or offended. Complaints fell into three groups: errors (things that never happened, diagnoses never discussed, personal details they had shared in confidence), labels ("elderly," "anxious," remarks on appearance or sexuality), and disrespect ("refused," "complains," "admits"). The authors recommend asking whether you'd accept the note if it were written about you. **[peer-reviewed]** ([Fernández et al., JGIM 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11606-020-06432-7))

**Categories that recur across all four professions:** name and what they like to be called; family members' names and key dates; food and drink preferences and allergies; how they like to communicate; hobbies, teams and interests; work role and history; what they're proud of and care about; logistics (travel, seating, room).

**What they hold back:** speculation and labels (clinicians), personal impressions (kept in a separate record), anything heard in confidence, and anything the person hasn't offered yet (gathered "at their comfort level"). They also control who can see the file ("not office gossip," "lock and key").

## 2. How AI memory products decide what to keep

- **ChatGPT** has two layers: explicit "saved memories," and since April 2025 a "reference chat history" layer that draws on all past chats. OpenAI says it "steers ChatGPT away from proactively remembering sensitive information, like health details, unless you explicitly ask." Users can view and delete memories, tell it to forget, or use Temporary Chat. **Deleting a chat does not delete memories made from it.** [vendor] ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/memory-and-new-controls-for-chatgpt/))
- **What ChatGPT actually stores** (80 users' GDPR data exports, 2,050 memories): **96% were created without the user asking**. 28% contain GDPR personal data and 7% special-category data, health most often. 52% hold mental-state content such as desires and intentions. On grounding, 77% were stated or paraphrased from the user, **14% inferred, 5% weakly supported and 4% unsupported**. **[peer-reviewed, WWW 2026]** ([Dash et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.01450))
- **Claude**: memory is kept per project. A "memory summary" can be viewed and edited, and Incognito chats neither read nor write memory. Anthropic says memory "focuses on learning your professional context," is "designed to avoid sensitive conversations and topics," and was tested for whether it "could reinforce harmful patterns ... [or] lead to over-accommodation." [vendor] ([Anthropic](https://claude.com/resources/articles/memory))
- **Gemini "Saved info"**: users add, edit and delete items in Personal context, and "Your saved info" usually appears under sources when it was used (Google notes the label can be wrong). [vendor] ([Google support](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/16413516))
- **Letta/MemGPT**: memory is a set of always-in-context **blocks** (label, description, value, character limit, e.g. 5,000), with a default `human` block for the user and a `persona` block for the agent. The agent edits them with tools, and a block can be marked `read_only`. [vendor docs] ([Letta](https://docs.letta.com/guides/agents/memory-blocks))
- **Mem0** pulls candidate facts out of each message pair. It finds the 10 most similar existing memories, and an LLM picks **ADD / UPDATE / DELETE / NOOP**. The graph version **marks contradicted facts invalid instead of deleting them**. Mem0 claims +26% over OpenAI memory on LoCoMo, but the table I could read shows 67.13 vs 63.79, which doesn't support that figure. The OpenAI baseline was also given all memories. [vendor preprint] ([Mem0](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19413v1))
- **Zep/Graphiti** stores time on two axes: `t_valid`/`t_invalid` for when a fact was true, and `t'_created`/`t'_expired` for when the system learned it. A contradicting fact closes the old edge by setting its `t_invalid` to the new edge's `t_valid`, so history is kept. Raw episodes are the lossless source, and every fact links back to one. On LongMemEval with GPT-4o: overall 60.2→71.2%, single-session-preference 20.0→56.7%, knowledge-update 78.2→83.3% (but **76.9→74.4% with gpt-4o-mini**), context ~115k→1.6k tokens. [vendor preprint] ([Zep](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.13956))

## 3. Benchmarks and the failure modes they find

- **LongMemEval** (500 questions; five abilities: extraction, multi-session reasoning, **knowledge updates**, temporal reasoning, **abstention**). Commercial assistants lose about 30% accuracy over long histories, ChatGPT 37% and Coze 64% compared with reading offline. On knowledge updates, ChatGPT scored 0.833 and Coze 0.208, and ChatGPT "tended to overwrite crucial information." Adding extracted facts to retrieval keys improved recall by 9.4% and accuracy by 5.4%. **An answer that mentions the old value is graded correct as long as it also gives the new one.** [preprint/academic] ([Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.10813))
- **PrefEval** (3,000 preference-query pairs, 20 topics). With no help, most models follow a stated preference **under 10% of the time after only 10 turns (~3k tokens)**; Claude 3.5 Sonnet scored 0.07. A **reminder** of the preference raises this to 0.45–0.98. Implicit preferences are harder than explicit ones. **[peer-reviewed, ICLR 2025]** ([Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09597v1))
- **PersonaMem** (20 personas, 180+ histories, 32k–1M tokens). Frontier models reach about **50%**. They recall facts and track how preferences changed reasonably well (60–70%), but do worse at **acting on the latest preference (30–50%)** and at recommendations and generalising to new situations. Accuracy falls the further a request is from where the preference was stated. **[peer-reviewed, NeurIPS 2025]** ([Jiang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14225v1))
- **LoCoMo** (50 dialogues of about 300 turns and 19 sessions each). LLMs trail humans by **56% overall and 73% on temporal questions**. Long-context models make up answers to unanswerable questions (GPT-3.5-16K scored 2.1 F1 on adversarial questions) and credit events to the wrong speaker. Retrieving **observations (assertions about each speaker) worked best**, beating session summaries 41.4 to 32.5 F1, and accuracy fell as more observations were retrieved. **[peer-reviewed, ACL 2024]** ([Maharana et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17753v1))
- **PersistBench** (500 samples, 18 models). Median rate of **cross-domain leakage** (an irrelevant memory shaping the answer) is **53%**. Median rate of **memory-induced sycophancy** is **~97.8%**. Filtering memories by relevance reduces both but makes models miss memories they should use. [preprint, ICML 2026 listing] ([Pulipaka et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.01146))
- **OP-Bench** (1,700 instances on LoCoMo users). **Every memory-augmented setup did worse than using no memory** (26–61% relative drop) on irrelevance, sycophancy (including agreeing with **memories that don't exist**) and repetition. Models paid more than twice as much attention to memory tokens as to the query. More elaborate systems (Mem0, MemOS) dropped more than simple RAG. [preprint] ([Hu et al.](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.13722.md))
- **MIT/Penn State field study** (38 users, two weeks, ~90 queries each). In 4 of 5 models, conversation context made the model more agreeable, and **a condensed user profile in memory had the largest effect**. Users said the models guessed their political views correctly only about half the time. [preprint; press summary] ([MIT EECS](https://www.eecs.mit.edu/personalization-features-can-make-llms-more-agreeable/), [paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.12517v2))

**Failure modes these sources name:** (1) stale values, where the old preference wins; (2) overwriting, where the old value is lost; (3) a one-off remark applied everywhere (leakage, "baiting"); (4) hallucinated or unsupported memories (4–5% in real ChatGPT data); (5) sycophancy driven by the profile; (6) repetition; (7) wrong speaker; (8) no abstention.

## 4. Do preferences last? Recency and change

- **End-of-history illusion** (6 studies, >19,000 people aged 18–68). People expect to change far less over the next decade than people a decade older say they changed. This held for personality, values and preferences, measured as **current favourite food, favourite music and best friend**. Predictors expected these to stay the same; reporters recalled big changes. **[peer-reviewed, Science 2013]** ([summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End-of-history_illusion))
- On the other hand, **vocational interests are very stable**: retests at 4, 8 and 12 years after college entry (n=409) show "a remarkable degree of interest stability," though stability varies from person to person. **[peer-reviewed, J. Vocational Behavior 1988]** ([Swanson & Hansen](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/stability-of-vocational-interests-over-4-year-8-year-and-12-year-/))
- So broad interest areas (e.g. "likes building things") last for years, while specific favourites (a dish, a band, a tool) turn over within years or months. A "favorite" should be treated as a dated snapshot, not a trait.
- How products handle it: Zep closes the old fact's validity interval and keeps it; Mem0's graph marks it invalid; LongMemEval accepts answers that mention the old value and lead with the new one; PersonaMem finds that models can **recall** the history but don't **act on** the latest value. "Newest wins, older kept as history" matches the strongest pattern here, but only if the newest value is put clearly in front of the model (PrefEval: reminders took accuracy from 0.07 to 0.45–0.98).
- No source here gives a clean test for **one-off vs lasting**. OP-Bench's "baiting" and PersistBench's leakage show the cost of treating a passing remark as a trait. The practical signals are explicit wording ("my favorite," "switched to," "always"), repetition across sessions, and the user confirming it.

## 5. Privacy and ethics

### Stated vs inferred
- **GDPR Art. 9(1)** special categories: racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade-union membership, genetic data, biometric data used for identification, health, and sex life or sexual orientation. Exceptions include **explicit consent for specified purposes (9(2)(a))** and data the person has **manifestly made public (9(2)(e))**. ([gdpr-info.eu](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/))
- In **C-184/20 (2022)** the CJEU held that data "liable **indirectly** to reveal" a special category, such as a partner's name revealing sexual orientation, counts as special-category data. **Inference can turn an ordinary fact into a sensitive one.** ([Covington](https://www.insideprivacy.com/eu-data-protection/special-category-data-by-inference-cjeu-significantly-expands-the-scope-of-article-9-gdpr/))
- **Wachter & Mittelstadt**: inferences are "economy class" personal data, with weak rights to see, correct or challenge them. They propose a **right to reasonable inferences** for high-risk inferences, which would require a justification up front: why the data supports the inference, why the inference is relevant, and whether the method is reliable. Their examples include inferred sexual orientation, depression, and pregnancy (Target). **[peer-reviewed, Columbia Business Law Review 2019]** ([Oxford blog summary](https://blogs.law.ox.ac.uk/business-law-blog/blog/2018/10/right-reasonable-inferences-re-thinking-data-protection-law-age-big))

### Creepiness
- **Target**: about 25 products combined into a "pregnancy prediction" score. A father learned of his teenage daughter's pregnancy from the coupons. Target then mixed baby ads with unrelated items because "as long as a pregnant woman thinks she hasn't been spied on, she'll use the coupons." This is the standard example of an inference the person never disclosed. [journalism] ([NYT via Columbia](https://moglen.law.columbia.edu/twiki/pub/CompPrivConst/HowCompaniesLearnOurConsumingSecrets/How_Companies_Learn_Your_Secrets_-_NYTimes.com.pdf))
- **What creepiness is**: three factors, **implied malice, undesirability (out of context), and unpredictability (can't anticipate or control it)**, measured with an 8-item scale (α=0.74, test-retest 0.82). **[peer-reviewed, CHI 2021]** ([Woźniak et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.07470))
- **Covert vs overt collection**: when data was collected openly, more personalised ads got more clicks. Covert collection reversed this, and click-through fell sharply once people learned their data was taken without consent. Feelings of vulnerability explained the effect. **[peer-reviewed, J. Retailing 2015]** ([Aguirre et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jouret/v91y2015i1p34-49.html))
- **Control raises acceptance**: after a social network gave users more privacy control, they were **nearly twice as likely to click personalised ads**, and the rise was biggest for ads using more unusual private information. **[peer-reviewed, JMR 2014]** ([Tucker](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/99170))
- **Transparency cuts both ways**: revealing "acceptable" flows (first-party data, **stated** attributes) on a trusted platform increased clicks, while revealing "unacceptable" flows (cross-site tracking, **inferred** attributes) reduced them. **[peer-reviewed, JCR 2019]** ([Kim, Barasz & John, via BU summary](https://www.bu.edu/susilo/2020/05/30/can-online-ad-transparency-enhance-ad-effectiveness)) A 2024 study likewise found that clearer disclosure sometimes made people *more* uncomfortable once they saw how much was tracked. **[peer-reviewed, J. Interactive Advertising]** ([UF summary](https://www.jou.ufl.edu/insights/personalized-ads-friend-foe-or-creepy-stalker/))
- **Chatbot users**: 68% of active ChatGPT users didn't know the memory bank existed. **91% said they'd use memory before seeing it, and 26% after.** For Gemini, 33% asked how to remove it. One user said their saved memories were "largely false." The most common explicit wish (22%) was **no personalization for work topics**. ChatGPT told 39% of users it didn't know their name even though they had given it. [preprint, venue not stated] ([Wang et al.](https://angelina-wang.infosci.cornell.edu/files/chatbot_personalization.pdf))
- The personalization-privacy paradox literature frames the trade-off as more value from personalization against privacy concern, shaped by context and presentation. The review I could reach was not peer-reviewed. [not peer-reviewed] ([Hsu et al. 2019](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/output/1492986/managing-the-personalization-privacy-paradox-a-systematic-literature-review))

### Caring vs surveillant, summarised
In this research, a remembered detail feels caring when the person **told you** it, it is **used in the context it came from**, it is **visible and correctable**, and it is **in proportion** to the relationship (Mackay's "comfort level"). It feels surveillant when it was **inferred**, shows up **in an unrelated context**, is **sensitive**, or the person **can't predict or control** it. The clinicians' test applies directly: would they be fine reading this note about themselves?

## Implications for people cards / interest notes

1. **Store only what the user stated, never inferences.** In real ChatGPT memory, 14% of entries were inferred and 4% unsupported, and inferred attributes are the "unacceptable" flow that makes transparency backfire. An interest note should hold the user's own words plus a source-turn pointer, the way Zep ties each fact to its episode.
2. **Keep special categories out of automatic writes.** Health, religion, politics, sexuality and the rest are written only when the user explicitly asks; C-184/20 means "indirectly revealing" counts too, e.g. a partner's name or "switched to my new meds." That follows OpenAI's and Anthropic's own policies.
3. **Use the bi-temporal shape you already planned**: `valid_from` (date stated), `superseded_at` (date of the next value), and `source_turn`. Newest wins, but keep the old value as history; LongMemEval counts "used to be X, now Y" as correct.
4. **Put only the current value in the packet; pull history only when asked.** PersonaMem shows models recall the history but fail to act on the latest value (30–50%). Two values in front of the model is a wrong-packet risk.
5. **Treat a favourite as a dated snapshot, not a trait.** Favourite food, music and best friend do change (end-of-history illusion); broad interest areas are stable (vocational-interest data). Show the date ("as of 2026-03") so the model and user can judge staleness, and consider decaying confidence on "favorite" notes after some months without mention.
6. **Write durable notes only from explicit wording.** "my favorite," "switched to," "always," "obsessed with" qualify; a passing "I had sushi today" does not. Leakage of one-off remarks is the most common failure (PersistBench median 53%; OP-Bench "baiting").
7. **Select by relevance, and let an empty packet be the default.** Every memory system on OP-Bench did worse than no memory on irrelevance and sycophancy, so a note goes into the packet only when the current turn's topic matches. This is "empty beats wrong" in practice.
8. **Watch profile-driven sycophancy.** A condensed user profile caused the most agreement bias (MIT), and PersistBench's median sycophancy failure was ~98%. Cards should hold facts and preferences, not the user's opinions on contested questions, or those notes should be gated harder.
9. **Make every card visible, editable and forgettable, and say when one was used.** Control nearly doubled acceptance (Tucker); covert use turned personalization against the product (Aguirre). A "used: interest note X" marker, like Gemini's "Your saved info" label, makes the system predictable, which removes one of the three creepiness factors.
10. **Keep notes in the context they came from.** 22% of chatbot users don't want personalization on work topics, and Claude keeps memory per project. Tag each note with its domain (work, home, hobby) and don't let it cross over unasked.
11. **People cards about third parties need stricter rules than the user's own card.** Those people never consented. Keep names, relationships and logistics the user stated; leave out their health, money and private matters unless the user explicitly asks (the Mackay and EA files guard these "under lock and key").
12. **Separate capture from commit, like Ritz-Carlton's pad and review step.** The automatic writer proposes a note and a stricter check (explicit wording, not sensitive, has a source turn) commits it. Anything uncertain stays in the lossless log, where it can still be found by search, instead of becoming a card.
